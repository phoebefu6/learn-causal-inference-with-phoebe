Learn Causal Inference with Phoebe · Leader session 1 of 6

Three questions, three rungs

Kestrelmark, a subscription retailer, sent a re-engagement email to 5,000 drifting customers. The ones who got it went on to spend 10.54 units more than the ones who did not. That number is correct, it reproduces every time the query runs, and it is not the effect of the email. The effect of the email is 6.23. The missing 4.31 is winter and customer tenure, two variables that were already sitting in the same table. Tonight is about the reason a dashboard could never have caught that, and about the one question a leader can ask in a meeting that separates a claim worth spending money on from a claim that only looks like one.

LiveWhy "did it work" is a harder question than it sounds3 min▶

When somebody asks whether the email worked, they are asking about a world that does not exist: the one where the same customers, in the same winter, with the same tenure, did not receive it. Nobody has data from that world. Every number on the slide comes from the world where some people got the email and some did not, and those two groups were never the same kind of people to begin with.

That is not a data quality problem and it does not get better with a bigger warehouse. It is a difference in the kind of question being asked. Some questions are about what tends to appear alongside what, and a table answers those completely. Some questions are about what would change if somebody acted, and a table cannot answer those on its own, no matter how clean it is. Telling the two apart takes about fifteen seconds once you know the shape, and it is the highest-leverage fifteen seconds in a data review.

Real world

Kestrelmark's growth team brought a slide reading "re-engagement email: +10.54 spend per customer" and asked for budget to roll it out to the whole base. The finance director asked one question: were the people who got it already the people who were going to spend more anyway. The room went quiet, because everybody knew the campaign had been sent in December to the longest-standing accounts.

Self-studyThe words that will keep coming back4 min▶
  • Association. Two things move together in the data. True, measurable, and silent about why.
  • Intervention. Somebody acts. The question is what changes as a result of the acting, not as a result of who tends to be acted upon.
  • Counterfactual. The world that did not happen. What this particular customer would have done if the email had never arrived.
  • Confounder. Something that causes both the action and the outcome. Winter causes more email and more spending, so winter is one.
  • Holding fixed. Comparing like with like: December buyers against December buyers, five-year accounts against five-year accounts. The whole craft is knowing what belongs on that list.
  • Estimand. The quantity you actually want a number for, written down in one sentence before anybody opens a tool. Most disputes about a result are really disputes about this, discovered late.
  • Bias against noise. Noise is being unsure of the right answer. Bias is being sure of the wrong one. More data fixes only the first.
Part 1 · covers the three rungs and what each one requires

Three rungs, and you cannot skip one

10 min live

Causal questions come in three kinds, and they form a ladder rather than a menu. Each rung asks something the rung below it cannot express, and each rung needs information the rung below it does not contain. That second half is the part that costs money, because a team can spend a quarter answering a rung-1 question beautifully while the decision on the table was rung 2 the whole time.

Rung 1Association: what does seeing X tell me about Y2 min▶

Rung 1 is the world of observed regularities. The questions here are about the joint distribution of things as they occur: how often do these two appear together, what is the average outcome among the people who did this, what is the rate in this segment against that one. Every one of them is answerable from a table, and answerable exactly, given enough rows.

This rung is not a lesser one. Almost all reporting lives here and it should: monitoring, segmentation, forecasting from patterns that keep holding, spotting that something changed. The mistake is never in asking a rung-1 question. The mistake is in accepting a rung-1 answer to a question that was asked on rung 2.

  • Kestrelmark's rung-1 question. "Among customers who received the email, what was the average spend, compared with those who did not?" Answer: 10.54 units higher. Correct and reproducible.
  • What it does not say. Anything about what would happen if the email were sent to someone new. The 10.54 describes two groups of people, not one action.
Rung 2Intervention: what happens if I do X3 min▶

Rung 2 asks for the probability of an outcome when a variable is forced to a value, rather than observed at that value. The difference between forcing and observing is the whole discipline. When you observe that a customer received the email, you also learn something about the kind of customer they are: probably a long-standing one, probably one the campaign calendar picked in December. When you force the email on everybody, you learn nothing about them, because the choice was yours and not the calendar's.

Almost every question that carries a budget is on this rung. Should we send it. Should we build it. Should we change the price. Should we keep paying for this channel. Each of them is a question about acting, and each of them requires a statement about what the action changes and what it leaves alone. That statement is a model of the situation, and it has to come from people who know the business, because no column in the warehouse contains it.

Real world

Kestrelmark's rung-2 question was "if we send this to the whole base next quarter, what happens to spend?" The honest answer, once winter and tenure were held fixed, was 6.56 per customer, against a true average effect of 6.23; the small residual is sampling noise rather than bias. At the scale being proposed, the difference between 10.54 and 6.23 was the difference between a campaign that paid for itself twice over and one that roughly broke even.

Rung 3Counterfactuals: would it have happened anyway2 min▶

Rung 3 asks about a world that did not happen, for a case that did. Not "what happens on average if we send the email" but "this customer received it and then bought; would she have bought without it?" That requires holding two worlds side by side for the same individual, only one of which was ever observed, and it can only be done from a fully specified model of how the outcome is produced.

This is the rung of blame, credit, and attribution, and it is why those conversations are so hard. "The campaign saved this account" is a rung-3 claim. So is "we would have hit the number anyway". Both are answerable in principle and neither is answerable from a comparison of groups.

  • Where leaders meet rung 3. Retention saves, churn prevention, incrementality of a discount to a customer who was going to buy, and any sentence containing the word "because" about a single case.
  • What it costs. Everything rung 2 needs, plus a specification detailed enough to run the same individual through both worlds. Practitioner session 6 does this properly for Kestrelmark.
The load-bearing ruleA rung-2 question cannot be answered with rung-1 information3 min▶

This is the formal result underneath the whole evening, and it is worth stating as flatly as it deserves. The information that lives on rung 1 - the joint distribution of everything you observed, in unlimited quantity - is not sufficient to answer a rung-2 question. Not hard to use, not noisy: insufficient. The same table is consistent with the email causing the spend, with high spenders being the ones selected for the email, and with December causing both. Choosing between those three requires something that is not in the table.

The practical consequence is uncomfortable and worth saying out loud in your next review. A dashboard cannot answer "did the email work", no matter how much data is behind it. Not a better dashboard, not a real-time one, not one with more dimensions. The limitation is in the kind of information a dashboard holds, not in its size or its freshness. What closes the gap is a statement about which things cause which - and that statement comes from the people who know how the campaign was targeted.

The tell in the room. When somebody answers a "what if we do X" question by describing what happened among people who already did X, they have quietly stepped down a rung. It is almost never dishonest. It is what the available evidence supports, and the step down goes unmentioned because nobody named the rungs.

Part 2 · covers the price of the mismatch and the triage that catches it

What the gap costs

7 min live

The abstract version of this is easy to nod along to. The version with a number attached is the one that changes a review process, so here is the number. Kestrelmark's naive comparison gives 10.54. The true effect of the email is 6.23. The 4.31 in between is season and tenure, and both were already columns in the same table as the outcome.

LiveWhy more data does not help3 min▶

The instinct when a number is questioned is to get more of it: a longer window, a bigger sample, the full base instead of a slice. That instinct is exactly right for one problem and exactly wrong for the other, and the two look identical on a slide.

  • Noise is the wobble around the right answer. It shrinks as the sample grows, which is why more data feels like the universal fix.
  • Bias is a systematic offset from the right answer. It does not shrink. Kestrelmark at 5,000 customers gives 10.54; Kestrelmark at a million customers gives 10.54 with much tighter error bars, which is a more confident wrong answer and a more persuasive slide.
  • The confidence interval is silent about this. It describes how much the estimate would move if you drew a different sample from the same flawed comparison. It has nothing to say about whether the comparison was the right one.

So a leader hearing "the sample was 900,000 customers" has learned that the error bars are small. They have learned nothing at all about whether the number is the effect of anything.

Real world

The rebuttal Kestrelmark's growth lead reached for was "this is the entire customer base, it is not a sample". That is true and it makes the situation worse rather than better: the whole population, compared the wrong way, gives the wrong answer with no uncertainty attached to hide behind.

LiveThe three-question triage you can run in a meeting3 min▶

No tooling, no notice, about ninety seconds. Ask them in order and stop at the first one that has no good answer, because the later questions do not matter until the earlier ones are settled.

  1. Which rung is this question on? Is somebody going to act on the answer by doing something differently? Then it is rung 2, whatever the slide says. If the answer only informs a description of what is happening, it is rung 1 and the reporting is appropriate.
  2. What would we have to hold fixed for this comparison to be fair? Name the things that push both who gets treated and what the outcome is. At Kestrelmark that list was two items long and took a minute to produce, from people who knew the campaign calendar.
  3. Do we have those things? Not "could we get them eventually": are they columns we can use for this analysis. If the list from question two contains something nobody measures, that is the finding, and it is a much more useful finding at the start than after a month.

What makes this work in a room is that all three questions are about the business rather than about statistics, so the people who can answer them are already at the table. Nobody has to defend a method. They have to say what drives who gets the email, which is something a campaign manager knows off the top of her head.

Self-studyMost questions are rung 2, most reporting is rung 14 min▶

This is the single most common mismatch in a data team's output, and it is nobody's fault in particular. Reporting infrastructure is built to describe what happened, because that is what can be built reliably and refreshed nightly. Decisions are about what to do next. The two are one rung apart and the seam between them is usually invisible, because the same numbers appear on both sides of it.

  • The symptom on the team's side. Analysts producing accurate work that keeps getting questioned, and not understanding why "but is it causal" arrives after every delivery. They answered the question they were given.
  • The symptom on the leader's side. A vague sense that the numbers are right but the decisions do not get easier. That feeling is the rung gap and it does not close by asking for more dashboards.
  • The cheap fix. Label the rung when the question is commissioned, not when the result is reviewed. One word in the request, and both sides know what would count as an answer.
  • The honest caveat. Labelling a question rung 2 does not make it answerable. Sometimes the correct outcome of the triage is "we cannot answer this from what we have, and here is what we would have to run instead". That is a good day, arriving early.

When the answer is that the data cannot support the claim, the next question is which method could - and estimating an effect from observational data belongs to learn-experimentation-with-phoebe, which covers potential outcomes, propensity scores and the rest of that machinery in working depth.

Build-along

Sort your questions

★ 20 min · everyone builds

Nothing to install and nothing to query. A page, a pen, and the last ten things your team was actually asked for. If you cannot recall ten, five real ones beat ten invented ones.

List the last ten questions your team was asked. Write them in the words the requester used, not the tidy version. "Why is churn up in the north" and "how much did the discount help" are the raw material; "churn analysis Q3" is a project name and tells you nothing about the rung.

Mark each one rung 1, rung 2 or rung 3. The test is what happens to the answer. If it describes or monitors, rung 1. If somebody will act differently as a result, rung 2. If it is about one specific case and what would have happened otherwise, rung 3. Where a question hides two questions, split it.

For every rung-2 question, write what would have to be held fixed. One line each. Ask what pushes both sides: what makes somebody more likely to receive the treatment, and what independently makes their outcome better or worse. Kestrelmark's line reads "season, tenure" and that was enough to move the answer by 4.31.

Beside each of those, mark whether you have it. Three marks are enough: have it, could get it, cannot measure it. The third mark is the valuable one and the one people are reluctant to write down.

Count how many were answered with rung-1 evidence. A group comparison, a segment average, a trend line, a correlation. This count is the output of tonight. In most teams it is high, and it is high for reasons that are structural rather than careless.

Pick the most expensive mismatch and say what you will do differently. Usually one of three things: reframe the question so rung 1 genuinely answers it, commission the holding-fixed properly, or accept that the honest answer needs a different kind of evidence entirely. Session 4 is about how to tell which.

★ The worksheet, one line per question, in whatever tool you already use question as it was asked | rung | hold fixed | have it? --------------------------------- | ---- | ------------------- | -------- did the re-engagement email work? | 2 | season, tenure | yes which segment churns most? | 1 | - | n/a should we discount the lapsed? | 2 | tenure, past value | yes would this account have left? | 3 | full model needed | no is signup down this month? | 1 | - | n/a did the new onboarding help? | 2 | cohort, channel | partly # count the rung-2 rows answered with a group comparison. # any "no" in the last column is a finding, not a blocker to hide.

Do this with the requesters in the room, not just the analysts. The rung of a question is a fact about what somebody intends to do with the answer, and only they know that. Ten minutes of sorting with the business in the room resolves arguments that survive months of back-and-forth over methods.

Homework

Before session 2

30 min
  1. Finish the ten-question worksheet and share it with the team that produces the answers. Ask them whether they agree with your rung labels; where you disagree, the disagreement is usually about what the requester meant, which is the useful conversation.
  2. Find one decision made in the last year on rung-1 evidence where the question was rung 2. Write down what was held fixed, what was not, and roughly what the decision was worth. You do not need to reopen it. You need one local example with a number on it.
  3. Take the biggest recurring report your team produces and write the rung beside its top three metrics. Notice how many of them are read as though they were on rung 2.
  4. Bring one published or internal claim with the word "drove", "led to" or "incremental" in it. Session 2 audits exactly that sentence, and it is much better with your own material.
Check yourself

Three questions

Your team reports that customers who received the re-engagement email spent 10.54 more. Someone proposes running it on the full base of a million customers to be sure. What happens to the 10.54?

Noise shrinks with sample size; a systematic offset does not. The whole population compared the wrong way gives the wrong answer with no uncertainty left to warn you.

A leader asks "what would spend be next quarter if we sent this email to everyone?" Which rung is the question on, and what does that require?

Forcing is not observing. Rung 3 would be about one named customer and the world where she did not receive it; a population-level "if we do X" is rung 2.

Why can a dashboard not answer "did the email work", however much data sits behind it?

The same table fits the email raising spend, high spenders being selected for the email, and December driving both. That is a limit on the kind of information available, not on the volume or the freshness.
