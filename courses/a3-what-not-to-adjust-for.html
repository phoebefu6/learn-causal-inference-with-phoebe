What not to adjust for

The instinct in every model review is that another control is a safety measure. It is not. On Kestrelmark's 5,000 customers, where the true effect of the re-engagement email is known to be 6.23, the correct two-variable adjustment lands at 6.56. Adding one variable that was measured before the email went out drops it to 5.26. Adding the mechanism itself gives 5.48. Adding a variable that was measured after the email drops it to 2.59. And a fourth variable, sitting in the same list, changes nothing at all. Tonight is about which is which, and about the one rule you can enforce without opening the model.

How the leader track works

Six sessions, 45 minutes each, no code and no query boxes. The output is not a model; it is the ability to read a causal claim, say what it assumes, and know when to send it back. Session 1 was the three rungs, session 2 was the claim audit, tonight is the covariate list, session 4 is whether the question is answerable at all, session 5 is why an average effect can be positive while the policy is wrong, and session 6 is what to require in writing when you commission the work. ★ What you walk out with tonight. Three named ways a well-intentioned control makes an estimate worse, each with the number it costs on one dataset where the truth is known. The finding that those three push the same direction and stack rather than cancelling. And one rule, enforceable in a review by somebody who does not fit models: every variable in the adjustment set has a named role, and "it improved the fit" is not a role.

LiveWhy the covariate list is a leadership artifact3 min▶

A covariate list is usually treated as a technical detail, delegated downward, approved on the grounds that it looks thorough. It is not a technical detail. It is the place where the analysis quietly decides which question it is answering, and the decision is invisible in the output: every one of the sets below produces a clean regression, a plausible coefficient and a defensible-looking table.

The reason this belongs to whoever commissioned the work is that the information needed to judge the list is not in the data. It is knowledge about how the business actually operates - what happens first, what causes what, what the scoring model was built from. That knowledge sits with the people who run the process, not with the person writing the query.

Real world

Kestrelmark's model spec went through review as a list of six variable names in a pull request, approved by two people who said it looked reasonable. Every set on this page looks reasonable, including the one that misses the true effect by 5.25. Nobody in that review was careless. The list simply cannot be read by eye, and nobody had told them that.

Self-studyFour words worth having exactly right4 min▶
  • Confounder - something that causes both the treatment and the outcome. Season and tenure at Kestrelmark: winter sends more email and sells more; long-tenured accounts get more email and buy more anyway. These are the variables that must be adjusted for.
  • Mediator - something on the causal route from treatment to outcome, carrying part of the real effect. Whether the customer opened the email. Adjusting for one is not an error; it changes the question.
  • Collider - something caused by two other things at once. Whether a support ticket was raised, which is caused both by getting the email and by spending more. Adjusting for one manufactures an association that was not there.
  • Bystander - something related to neither. The customer's device. Harmless, and worth knowing about because it proves the other three cannot be spotted by feel.

These four words are the whole vocabulary for tonight. A leader who can ask "which of these four is it" for every line in a covariate list has most of the value of the technique.

Part 1 · covers the three harms and what each one costs

Adding a control is not a safe default

10 min live

Three shapes account for nearly every harmful control in applied work. They are distinguishable on a diagram in seconds and indistinguishable in a list of column names. Each one below is measured on the same 5,000 Kestrelmark customers, against the same true effect of 6.23, starting from the correct adjustment set that gives 6.56.

LiveThe mediator: 5.48, and an answer to a different question4 min▶

Kestrelmark's email works partly because people open it and then buy. Whether the customer opened is therefore on the causal route, carrying part of the effect the campaign is being credited with. Hold it fixed and the estimate falls from 6.56 to 5.48, a bias of 0.75 against the true total effect of 6.23.

Notice what 5.48 actually is. The direct part of the effect, averaged over the customers in the model that generated this data, is 5.16. The analyst who adjusted for opening has not miscalculated anything. She has measured "what does the email do for somebody who never opens it", accurately, and reported it under a heading that promised the total effect.

  • Total effect answers "should we send the email". Nearly always the business question, and 6.23 is its answer.
  • Direct effect answers "how much survives if nobody opens it". A real question for a subject-line project, and 5.16 is its answer.
  • Nothing in the output distinguishes them. Both are a coefficient with a small standard error and a sensible sign.

The leadership version of this rule. Ask which effect the brief asked for before anybody chooses variables, and put the answer in one sentence at the top of the brief. A mediator in the list is only an error relative to a question, and if the question was never written down, no reviewer can call it.

LiveThe post-treatment collider: 2.59, and the most expensive line on the list4 min▶

A support ticket at Kestrelmark has two causes. Getting the email prompts questions, so the email raises the ticket rate. Spending more also prompts questions, so higher spend raises it too. Two arrows point into one box, and that box is a collider. Adjusting for it moves the estimate to 2.59, a bias of 3.64, and that is by a wide margin the largest single harm on this bench.

The rule behind this is worth stating in its general form, because it applies far outside analytics. In a structure i -> m <- j, conditioning on m makes i and j dependent when they were independent. Epidemiologists call it Berkson's paradox, after the observation that among hospital patients two unrelated diseases appear correlated: each one is a reason to be admitted, so knowing a patient is admitted and does not have the first raises the chance they have the second.

The same machinery runs at Kestrelmark. Among customers who raised a ticket, one who did not get the email probably spent a lot, because something had to prompt the ticket. Conditioning on the ticket has invented a relationship between the email and spend that the business never created, and it pushes the estimate down.

Real world

Post-treatment colliders enter model specs because they correlate beautifully with the outcome. Support contact, app sessions after the campaign, whether the customer reached the checkout: all measured after the intervention, all strongly related to spend, all attractive to anybody optimising fit. The fit does improve. The answer gets worse.

LiveThe pre-treatment collider: 5.26, and the end of the timing defence4 min▶

This is the one that surprises experienced teams. Kestrelmark's loyalty score is computed monthly, long before the campaign is decided, from behaviour that predates the email entirely. By the usual folk rule it is safe: it happened first, so it cannot have been affected by the treatment. Adjust for it and the estimate falls to 5.26, a bias of 0.97. That is the middle of tonight's three harms by size, and it is still bias, arriving from a variable the folk rule certified as safe.

The reason is the shape, not the clock. Two things nobody measured feed into that score. One of them is the disposition that also makes a customer a target for the campaign; the other is the disposition that also makes them a spender. The loyalty score is their common effect, so conditioning on it links the two unmeasured causes, and through them links the email to spend by a route that has nothing to do with the campaign. The shape is written A <- U1 -> C <- U2 -> Y and it is known as M-bias, after the letter the diagram draws.

  • "It was measured before the treatment" is not a safety argument. It rules out one specific problem and says nothing about this one. Being far from the largest harm on the bench does not make it a small one.
  • Composite scores are the usual carriers. A propensity-to-churn score, a health score, an engagement tier: each one is built from many inputs, which is exactly what makes it a common effect of many things.
  • The unmeasured causes are what make it dangerous, which means no amount of inspecting the score itself will reveal the problem. It is a question about how the score was built.
Self-studyWhy the diagram is the only place these are visible4 min▶

All three harms are statements about the direction of arrows, and direction is precisely what a dataset does not contain. Two columns that move together are consistent with the first causing the second, the second causing the first, and a third thing causing both. No statistic separates those, at any sample size, which is why a correlation matrix cannot tell you whether a variable belongs in an adjustment set.

What separates them is knowledge of the business: what happens first, what the scoring model was fitted on, which team's process generates the field. That knowledge is held by operators, not by the analyst, and the diagram is simply the format that lets an operator contribute it in twenty minutes without learning any statistics.

The twenty-minute version. Put the treatment and the outcome on a whiteboard. For every variable in the proposed adjustment set, ask the operator two questions: does this affect who gets treated, and is this affected by the treatment or by the outcome. The first question finds the confounders you need. The second finds the harms.

Part 2 · covers why the list looks fine and what to enforce instead

Why the list cannot be read by eye

9 min live

If harmful variables looked harmful, review would work. The reason it does not is a pair of results on the same bench: one variable in the list costs nothing whatsoever, and the three that cost something all push in the same direction and add up.

LiveThe bystander result: device changes nothing3 min▶

The customer's device is in the same table, was written by the same pipeline, and looks exactly as respectable as the loyalty score. Add it to the correct adjustment set and the estimate is 6.56, with a bias of 0.33. That is the back-door set's own answer, unchanged to two decimal places. It is related to neither the email nor spend, so holding it fixed costs nothing.

That result is not a footnote. It is the reason the other three are dangerous. A covariate list contains free variables and expensive ones, and the free ones are not marked. Somebody reading six column names and asking whether they seem sensible will pass all six, because five of them are sensible-looking and one of them genuinely is harmless.

What this rules out as a review method. Judging the list by plausibility, by how well the model fits, by whether the coefficient looks stable, or by whether an experienced person nods at it. All four of those pass the 5.25 set. Only the roles distinguish them.

LiveThe compounding result: 0.97 plus 0.75 plus 3.64 becomes 5.254 min▶

The natural hope is that a long list is a hedge: if one variable is wrong, surely the others dilute it, and the errors partly wash out. On this bench they do not. Each harm alone costs 0.97, 0.75 and 3.64. All three together give an estimate of 0.98, a bias of 5.25, which is close to their sum and nowhere near zero. They push the same direction and they stack.

On this data the cautious-sounding list is worse than no list at all. Adjusting for everything costs 5.25; adjusting for nothing costs 4.31. A team that controlled for every column it had would have been further from the truth than a team that ran the raw comparison and thought about nothing, because the post-treatment collider alone gives up 3.64.

The comparison worth quoting, though, is not one error against another. It is bias against noise. Regenerate the data under five different random draws and the correct set's residual is +0.33, +0.17, -0.38, +0.19, -0.01, averaging +0.06. It changes sign, which is what sampling noise does. Every contaminated set keeps the same sign in all five draws: loyalty about -1.2, opened about -1.0, ticket about -3.7, everything about -5.2. A wrong adjustment is bias, and it does not shrink when the sample grows. The correct one leaves noise, which does.

  • A longer list is not insurance. Each additional variable is a separate bet that its role is benign, and the losing bets accumulate.
  • The right set was two columns everybody already had. Season and tenure. The work was knowing which two, not collecting more.
  • Both directions are available. Confounding pushed the estimate up by 4.31; the three harms pushed it down by 5.25. A team cannot even assume they are being conservative.
LiveSimpson's paradox: the version that reverses the sign3 min▶

Everything above shrinks or inflates a number. There is a harsher version in which the number changes sign: an effect that is positive in every subgroup is negative overall, or the reverse. That is Simpson's paradox, and it is the same mechanism at a magnitude that cannot be explained away as noise.

What makes it a leadership problem rather than a curiosity is that both tables are correct arithmetic. The aggregate is right, each stratum is right, and they disagree. Choosing which one to act on is not a calculation; it is a decision about the causal structure, and the diagram is what settles it. Practitioner session 5 reproduces the reversal from real numbers and works through which table to believe and why.

Real world

The board-deck form is a slide showing a treatment beating control overall, and an appendix where the same treatment loses in every segment. Both charts are produced by the same pipeline from the same data. The question "which of these is the causal answer" has a definite answer, and it does not come from the data.

LiveThe one rule to enforce: every variable has a named role3 min▶

None of this requires a leader to fit a model, read code or evaluate a standard error. It requires one rule, applied to every causal analysis the team ships: each variable in the adjustment set carries a written role - confounder, mediator, collider, or bystander - and one sentence saying why. Anything that cannot be labelled is labelled unknown and blocks sign-off until it is resolved.

  • "It improved the fit" is not a role. Fit improves fastest for variables caused by the treatment or the outcome, which is to say for the harmful ones.
  • "It was in the standard feature set" is not a role. Standard sets are assembled for prediction, where anything correlated with the outcome is welcome. Different job, opposite rules.
  • "It was measured before the treatment" is not a role. The loyalty score was, and it still costs 0.97. The most expensive variable on the bench, the support ticket at 3.64, is the one measured after.
  • "We adjusted for everything to be safe" is not a role. On this data it is worse than adjusting for nothing: 5.25 of bias against 4.31.

The rule is cheap to enforce and it is self-teaching. A team asked to name roles for six months stops including variables it cannot name, which removes most of the harm before anybody reaches a review.

Build-along

Read a covariate list

★ 18 min · everyone builds

No code and no model. Bring the variable list from one causal analysis your team currently ships - a campaign readout, a feature impact study, a pricing claim. If you have nothing to hand, use Kestrelmark's six: season, tenure, loyalty score, opened, ticket, device.

Get the actual list, not the summary of it. Ask for the variables the model controls for, by name, as they appear in the code. Not "we control for the usual customer attributes". The harm lives in individual names, and a summary is where individual names go to hide.

Write the suspected role beside every variable. Five options: confounder, mediator, collider, bystander, unknown. Do this yourself, from business knowledge, before asking the analyst. You are testing whether the list is legible to somebody who knows how the business runs, which is the standard it has to meet.

Count the unknowns. That count is the honest measure of how much of the analysis is running on habit. A list of nine variables with six unknowns is not a rigorous model with some loose ends; it is three decisions and six coin flips.

Find anything measured after the treatment. Go through the list asking one question per variable: could the treatment have changed this? Anything with a yes is either a mediator or a post-treatment collider, and both change the answer. This single sweep catches most of the damage in most real lists.

Check every composite score against how it was built. Loyalty tiers, health scores, propensity scores, engagement grades. Ask what the score was fitted on. A score built from many behaviours is a common effect of many causes, which is the collider shape by construction, and timing does not rescue it.

Send the list back with the two questions that matter. First: which effect was the brief asking for, the total or the direct? Second: what is the role of each variable, in one sentence each? Ask for the answers in writing and attached to the analysis, not in a meeting.

★ The same list with a role beside every line, which is the whole ask Estimand: TOTAL effect of the email on 90-day spend. Nothing else. Unknowns: 0. Anything unknown blocks sign-off until it is named.

Keep the roles beside the query, not in a slide. A role list in a deck describes last quarter's model. A role list in the repository next to the code gets reviewed when somebody adds a variable, and the person adding it has to write the role to get it merged. That is the whole enforcement mechanism, and it costs one line per variable.

Homework

Before session 4

30 min
  1. Finish the role list for one shipped analysis and count the unknowns. Send the count to the person who owns the model, with no commentary attached. The number does the work.
  2. Pick the one composite score in your organisation that appears in the most models - the churn score, the health score, the engagement tier - and find out what it was fitted on. Write down whether it is a common effect of things that also drive your treatments.
  3. Find one analysis where a variable measured after the intervention is in the adjustment set. Decide whether the brief wanted the total effect or the direct effect, and write which, in one sentence, at the top of the brief.
  4. Bring one analysis where you suspect a back-door path runs through something nobody measures. Session 4 is about what to do when that is true, and the answer is not always "collect more data".
Check yourself

Three questions

Kestrelmark's loyalty score was computed a month before the campaign was decided. Adjusting for it moves the estimate from 6.56 to 5.26. Why does the timing not protect it?

This is M-bias, in the shape A <- U1 -> C <- U2 -> Y. Preceding the treatment rules out one problem and says nothing about being a common effect, which is why the pre-treatment collider still costs 0.97, even though it is not the largest of tonight's three harms.

A team argues that including all six variables is the cautious choice, since errors in individual controls should partly offset. On this data, all three harmful controls together give a bias of 5.25 while each alone costs 0.97, 0.75 and 3.64. What does that show?

5.25 is close to the sum of the three and nowhere near zero: every one of them pushes the estimate down, so nothing offsets. Dropping the worst would still leave the other two. And the errors do not stop at each other: adjusting for everything costs 5.25 against 4.31 for adjusting for nothing at all.

Adding the device variable to the correct adjustment set leaves the estimate at 6.56, unchanged to two decimal places. What is the leadership lesson in that non-result?

The bystander being genuinely harmless is what makes the other three invisible. Six respectable-looking column names, one of which costs nothing and three of which cost 0.97, 0.75 and 3.64.
